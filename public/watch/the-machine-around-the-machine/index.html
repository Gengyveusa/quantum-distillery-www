What the hardware does

Why does a superconducting quantum computer need a refrigerator?

Heat can push a qubit out of its starting state. A dilution refrigerator cools the chip to millikelvin temperatures, while shields and carefully anchored signal cables limit the heat and microwave noise that reach it.

ETH Zürich’s cryogenic measurements ↗

What does helium-3 have to do with the Moon?

Helium-3 helps carry heat away at the coldest stage of a dilution refrigerator. Bluefors agreed to buy up to 10,000 liters per year from Interlune, which plans to recover it from lunar soil. The announced delivery window is 2028–2037. Lunar extraction and delivery are still planned.

Bluefors agreement ↗How the cold stage works ↗

How does a neutral-atom quantum computer use lasers?

Lasers cool individual atoms, hold them in optical tweezers and illuminate them so a camera can detect which sites are occupied. A Caltech team trapped over 6,100 atoms in about 12,000 optical sites. The atoms are cold even when the surrounding apparatus operates at room temperature.

Caltech neutral-atom experiment ↗NIST/JILA optical tweezer ↗

Who else needs equipment this precise?

NIST uses a dilution refrigerator to cool a detector that measures energy from radioactive decay. Its ytterbium optical clocks use stabilized laser light to measure an atomic transition. The two instruments illustrate how cryogenics and precision optics serve other laboratories.

A refrigerator maker looks to the Moon

PeterWhy would a refrigerator company order helium from the Moon?

Thad ConnellyI’m Thad Connelly. This is The Quantum Distillery.

PeterIn September 2025, Bluefors agreed to buy up to 10,000 liters of helium-3 a year from Interlune, which plans to recover it from lunar soil. Deliveries are supposed to start in 2028. Bringing it back is still ahead of them. The refrigerators already exist. And if you want to understand that order, follow the helium.

There are two pieces of hardware to keep in mind while we follow this. One fits in the palm of your hand. It’s the package holding a quantum processor. The other can occupy a lab. It creates the conditions in which that first piece can do any work.

You can learn a surprising amount about the future of quantum computing from the person who has to make room for both. That person has to figure out where to put the pumps, how to get signals in, and how to tell whether the machine did what you asked.

01:02

Find the chip

Last time we counted the qubits it might take to break encryption. Today, look at the famous photograph of a superconducting quantum computer. Gold plates, loops of cable, something that looks like a chandelier hung upside down. Where’s the processor?

Follow the cables to the bottom. The chip occupies a small package down there. Much of what you’re looking at is the refrigerator. The photograph usually shows it open, so we can see inside. To run the machine, the team puts shields and a vacuum enclosure around those layers.

Picture a cross section through a series of nested metal cans. The outermost one faces a room full of warm surfaces. Shields farther in see colder surroundings. Cables have to get through all of it. And the chip at the bottom needs a good thermal connection to that last cold stage.

A thermometer on a metal plate can tell you the temperature of the plate. Finding out what the qubit is actually experiencing takes a measurement of the qubit itself.

02:03

A qubit has to start somewhere

Inside that chip, two energy levels of a tiny electrical circuit stand for zero and one. A pulse of microwave energy lets you change the state deliberately. Heat can give the circuit enough energy to change state all by itself. Suppose you tell it to start a calculation at zero. If it’s already bounced up to one, you’ve got the wrong answer to a question you haven’t even asked yet. Cooling makes those accidental excitations much less likely.

Take a representative superconducting qubit that responds at 5 gigahertz. Five billion cycles per second sounds fast, but a photon at that frequency carries very little energy. Express the gap between zero and one on a temperature scale and it’s about a quarter of a kelvin.

A room is roughly 300 kelvin. So the chip has to sit far below that quarter-kelvin scale, and its electrical surroundings have to be quiet at that frequency. You could have an excellent refrigerator and still spoil an experiment by giving the warm room a direct microwave path to the chip. That explains some of those little boxes mounted along the cables.

03:15

The helium goes around again

Now count the plates from top to bottom. Each stage intercepts heat before it reaches the next. At the cold end, a dilution refrigerator can get within a hundredth of a degree of absolute zero. The last step uses two forms of helium. Where the liquids meet, helium-3 can pass into a mixture rich in helium-4. That takes energy from the surroundings. Pumps keep the helium circulating so the cooling continues.

The same helium goes around again. Each additional fridge needs its own supply. Helium-3 is an isotope. Compared with the common helium-4 atom, its nucleus has one fewer neutron. That small difference changes how the two liquids behave near absolute zero and makes this cooling cycle possible.

Bluefors actually lists the gas quantities for different refrigerators. For one model, the listed charge is 40 liters of helium-3 and 180 liters of helium-4. Those are gas quantities, measured as gas volumes. Once the system is running, it circulates a fixed inventory.

So when a manufacturer lines up a future source, think about outfitting a fleet of machines. A year’s worth of computing on one machine is a different question.

04:37

Follow one cable

So we’ve put the chip in a quiet, cold place. Now we have to talk to it. Follow one control cable from the electronics rack, which is warm enough to rest your hand on, all the way down to the chip. It carries the microwave instruction. It also conducts heat.

Engineers fasten the cable to successive cold stages and reduce the noise coming down the line. Even when the computer is sitting idle, that cable is still bringing heat in.

A team in Zürich measured what was available at the bottom of a particular fridge. With one circulation setting they could remove about five millionths of a watt at 13 millikelvin. Let that stage warm to 20 millikelvin and it could remove about thirteen millionths of a watt. Same fridge. Same circulation setting. Two different answers to the question, how much cooling do I get?

The engineers also measured the heat conducted by cables and the heat released while electrical signals were running through them. If you’re adding a bundle of lines to that machine, you need both sets of numbers before you drill another hole.

05:47

What fits inside the fridge?

Imagine adding a hundred more control connections. Where do you put them, and how do you cool them? A Delft company called FrostByte is developing switches that work inside the refrigerator. The idea is to send signals down fewer lines and route them closer to the processor.

Put electronics in the cold and you face the next question. Those electronics generate heat of their own. How much can you dissipate on the stage where the switch sits? That’s a design decision you can measure in the lab.

Another team actually sent a control signal down an optical fiber. At the cold end a photodetector converted modulated laser light into a microwave signal the circuit could use. The researchers demonstrated that they could control and read a superconducting qubit over that link.

One qubit. That experiment answered a specific question. Could the conversion preserve a signal clean enough to operate the device? A machine with many such links raises additional questions about total optical power, heat from the conversion, and how many signals can share each fiber. A working experiment gives you a place to start measuring all three.

07:01

A different kind of cold

Walk into a neutral-atom lab and you can build a very different picture. Here an individual atom carries the information. It’s moving through a vacuum chamber, and you’d like it to stay put. Laser light slows the atoms down. A tightly focused beam can hold one in a tiny spot, an optical tweezer. Make an array of those spots, and you can arrange the atoms in rows.

The atoms themselves get extraordinarily cold. The chamber around them can stay at room temperature.

Look at what the Caltech team did in 2025. They made about 12,000 trap sites and loaded more than 6,100 neutral atoms. A trap site is a place the optics can hold an atom. A loaded site actually contains one. Take a picture and you can see which seats are occupied.

In the same work the researchers measured how well the atomic qubits preserved phase information, and they could pick up atoms, move them, and put them down while preserving coherence. Getting that combination right is what makes moving atoms around interesting for a future computation. The two counts tell you how many places they made and how many atoms they caught.

08:16

Read a row of atoms

Light gives you a way to read the answer, too. Shine light tuned to a particular atomic transition. An atom in the state you’re looking for scatters photons, and a lens collects some of them for a camera. On the image you can pick out individual bright sites.

Now the shopping list looks different. A laser that holds its frequency. Optics that focus it on the right site. A camera sensitive enough to collect that faint signal.

There’s a lovely practical catch. To see an atom clearly, you’d like to collect plenty of light. Scattering light kicks the atom, and enough kicks can knock it out of its trap. You want an image and you’d like your atom back when the exposure ends.

The Caltech group reported better than 99.99% imaging fidelity and measured survival separately. Try measuring both on an array of thousands. A camera that confidently reports an empty site and an atom that actually left the site can give you the same dark pixel. Figuring out which event happened is part of building the instrument.

09:23

Who else uses the cold?

Here’s a question for a supplier. If the big quantum computer comes five years late, who still needs your equipment? At the U.S. national standards lab, researchers use a dilution refrigerator to cool a radiation detector below five hundredths of a degree above absolute zero. A single radioactive decay deposits a tiny pulse of heat. From that pulse they can work out how much energy the decay released.

That’s an instrument doing a job today. There’s a second customer for precision optics, too. In an optical atomic clock, scientists tune a laser to a transition inside an atom. The transition gives them a reference for how fast the light is oscillating. The challenge is holding that laser steady enough to compare its frequency with the atoms over and over again.

The national standards lab runs clocks using laser-cooled atoms held in a lattice of light. Think back to the atom computer. People in those two labs can have a very specific conversation about laser stability, optics and control electronics. They can need some of the same engineering even when only one of them wants to run an algorithm.

10:39

Read the order closely

So go back to the Moon deal and read the words “up to.” They matter as much as the number beside them. A customer has put a figure and a delivery window on what it might buy. A space company still has to collect the gas and bring it home. And the atom lab we just visited can order another set of lasers without moving that helium order by a single liter.

To evaluate one of these suppliers, ask to see a customer acceptance test. For a refrigerator, what temperature did it hold with the customer’s wiring installed and the signals turned on? For a laser, how long did it stay within the required frequency range during a real run?

Then look at the second customer’s requirements. Sometimes a second lab needs nearly the same piece of equipment. Sometimes it needs so much redesign that it’s effectively a second product. That difference tells you much more about the business than a long list of industries on a slide.

11:40

The machine around the machine

One last look at those two photographs. On one side, a cable carries a microwave pulse down through a stack of metal plates. On the other, a lens brings light to a spot small enough to hold a single atom.

In each photograph it’s possible to point to where the information sits. It’s also possible to follow a signal all the way from an ordinary computer to that spot and back again. That whole trip has to work before anybody can run the algorithm. It brings the qubit count from the last episode into perspective.

The next time somebody shows you a quantum chip, ask them to widen the shot. Where does the heat go? What carries the instruction in? What carries the answer out? Those questions will tell you a surprising amount about what it takes to build the next one.

This has been The Machine Around the Machine. Thanks for joining us at The Quantum Distillery.
